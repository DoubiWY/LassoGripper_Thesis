\begin{abstract}
\addchaptertocentry{\abstractname}
\bigskip

This thesis studies tendon-driven mechanisms for adaptive robotic manipulation in constrained environments. It concentrates on four mechanism-level challenges: predictable tension transmission, dexterous motion with low distal mass, compliant distributed contact, and reliable operation under tight geometric and ergonomic constraints.

Two prototype embodiments validate the proposed design framework. First, Lasso Gripper is a string-loop end-effector that forms an adaptive capture region via controlled launch-and-retract of a tensile element. We present the mechanical design, control strategy, and demonstration experiments that show the concept's feasibility and practical advantages over conventional antipodal grippers: an extended capture envelope, distributed contact that reduces localized stress on soft objects, and robustness to positional uncertainty. The experiments reported are demonstration-focused and qualitative; they illustrate mechanism behaviors and common failure modes rather than providing exhaustive statistical metrics.

Second, a compact handheld motorized laparoscopic instrument translates the same tendon-driven principles into a clinically constrained form factor. A benchtop prototype integrating proximal actuation, differential tendon routing, tension-preserving reels, and closed-loop control is described and validated in interaction and interface tests. Clinical evaluation are beyond this work's scope and are left for future study.

Together, these contributions establish mechanism-level design rules for tendon-driven systems and demonstrate their transferability between general robotic grasping and minimally invasive surgical tools.
\end{abstract}
