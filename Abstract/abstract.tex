\bigskip
\noindent This thesis investigates tendon-driven mechanisms for robotic manipulation, with emphasis on four mechanism-level challenges: maintaining stable tension transmission, achieving dexterous motion with low distal mass, preserving compliant and adaptive contact, and operating reliably under geometric constraints. Rather than treating grasping and surgical instrumentation as separate topics, the thesis develops a common tendon-driven design framework and validates it through two physical embodiments.

The first embodiment is the Lasso Gripper, a novel string-driven end-effector inspired by the traditional lasso and the uurga. Instead of relying on concentrated antipodal contact, the proposed mechanism launches and retracts a string loop to form an adaptive capture region around the target. This design expands the effective capture range, distributes contact force along the tensile element, and improves the handling of oversized, irregularly shaped, and fragile objects. The gripper is realized through a dedicated launch-and-retraction mechanism, coordinated motor actuation, and a control pipeline that supports loop deployment, tightening, and manipulation. Experimental validation demonstrates successful grasping of diverse static and dynamic targets, including animal figures, vegetables, balloons, and moving objects, confirming the advantages of tension-based adaptive grasping over conventional rigid-finger approaches.

Beyond demonstrating a new gripper, the Lasso Gripper serves as a mechanism study platform from which general design principles are extracted. These principles include proximal actuation for reducing distal inertia, differential tendon routing for compact multi-degree-of-freedom behavior, and explicit tension management for improving repeatability and force transmission. The thesis then translates these principles into a second embodiment: a handheld motorized laparoscopic instrument for minimally invasive surgery.

The proposed surgical instrument employs two input motors, two magnetic sensors, and four output actuators to realize multiple distal degrees of freedom while preserving a hand-held form factor. Its tendon routing and reel architecture are designed to avoid slack accumulation and maintain continuous cable tension, while its sensing and control stack combines filtering and closed-loop motor feedback to suppress disturbance and improve motion fidelity. In this way, the thesis demonstrates how a tendon-driven mechanism framework can be transferred from adaptive robotic grasping to a clinically constrained manipulation task.

Taken together, the two systems establish a continuous thesis contribution at the mechanism level: tendon-driven designs can be systematically engineered to achieve adaptive grasping, dexterous manipulation, and safe interaction across both general robotic and surgical domains.
