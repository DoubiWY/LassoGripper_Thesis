\chapter{Future Works} % Main chapter title

\label{Chapter10} % For referencing the chapter elsewhere, use
The future directions of this thesis follow the same two-embodiment structure as the main body. The first line of work extends Lasso Gripper, the primary mechanism platform of the thesis, toward broader manipulation scenarios and more capable loop-based grasping strategies. The second line of work develops the tendon-driven surgical instrument toward higher precision and richer modularity under clinical constraints. In both cases, the underlying aim is to deepen the mechanism framework developed in this thesis and expand the range of tasks that tendon-driven systems can perform reliably.

\section{Further Applications of Lasso Gripper}
This section extends the adaptive grasping side of the thesis. The goal is not simply to add new demonstrations, but to probe how the loop-based mechanism can be scaled, coordinated, and deployed in more dynamic environments.

Future investigations may involve utilizing multiple loops (two or three) to grasp the same object, potentially enhancing gripping stability and robustness. This multi-loop approach could provide additional security and adaptability in handling complex shapes and sizes, offering a promising avenue for further development.
\subsection{Multi-Loop Configuration}
Implementing a multi-loop configuration involves arranging two or more string loops to work in tandem. Each loop would be controlled independently but coordinated through a central control system to ensure synchronized movement and force application. This setup is particularly advantageous for gripping objects with irregular geometries or those requiring more secure handling.
\subsection{Improved Gripping Stability}
The use of multiple loops can significantly improve gripping stability. By distributing the gripping force across several contact points, the likelihood of object slippage or deformation is reduced. This is especially beneficial for delicate or fragile items that might be damaged by excessive pressure from a single loop.
\subsection{Enhanced Adaptability}
Multiple loops could improve adaptability when handling a wide range of object sizes and shapes. Each loop could adjust its position and tension dynamically, conforming to the object's contours. This flexibility would allow Lasso Gripper to handle tasks that single-loop systems may struggle with, such as picking up objects with protrusions or indentations.
\subsection{Mobile Platform Grasping}
As demonstrated in Chapter 3, Lasso Gripper can capture selected flying objects under controlled setup conditions because of its rapid retraction capability. This mechanism could be extended to applications involving object securing and transportation. Multiple loops may increase the likelihood of catching and securing airborne objects by providing several possible capture paths.
\subsection{Potential Challenges and Solutions}
While the multi-loop approach offers potential advantages, it also introduces several challenges. Coordinating the movement and tension of multiple loops demands sophisticated control algorithms and real-time feedback systems. Additionally, the mechanical design must prevent interference among the loops during operation. Capturing randomly flying objects would further require the grasping algorithm to be robust to external obstacles such as walls or floors.

To address these challenges, future research should focus on control algorithms that integrate sensor data for dynamic adjustment of loop position and tension. Moreover, the mechanical design could incorporate features for maintenance and quick reconfiguration. These extensions would further strengthen the central claim of this thesis: tendon-driven mechanisms can be adapted to very different manipulation problems, provided that tension control, routing strategy, and task-specific interaction geometry are designed together.

\subsection{Quantitative Evaluation of Loop-Based Capture}

The demonstrations with Lasso Gripper in this thesis intentionally focused on feasibility and representative operating modes. A more complete evaluation should convert these demonstrations into a parameterized experiment matrix. Important independent variables include loop length, launch speed, retraction speed, approach angle, target mass, target size, surface friction, and the location of the capture region relative to the center of mass. Dependent variables should include capture success rate, time to secure the object, maximum tension, slippage distance, visible object deformation, and release reliability.

This protocol would make it possible to distinguish between three mechanisms of failure. A geometric failure occurs when the loop does not pass around a valid caging or neck-like region. A dynamic failure occurs when the loop reaches the object but collapses or tightens too late. A stability failure occurs after initial capture, when the object rotates or slips out during lifting, as observed in the chili-pepper demonstration. Separating these failure types would provide clearer design feedback than a single binary success metric.

Future work should also compare Lasso Gripper with conventional antipodal and enveloping grippers using matched target sets. The comparison should not only report success rate, but also document object deformation, required pose accuracy, tolerance to target motion, and maximum object dimension relative to gripper size. These metrics are better aligned with the proposed mechanism advantage of Lasso Gripper: it is not intended to replace every rigid gripper, but to extend capture range and reduce contact concentration for objects that are oversized, deformable, moving, or difficult to approach precisely.

\section{Surgical Instruments}
While the current prototype of the cable-driven surgical instrument demonstrates basic functional feasibility, there are opportunities to improve precision, adaptability, and overall system intelligence. Future work should focus on the following areas:

\subsection{Enhanced Precision through Adaptive Learning-based Control}

A fundamental limitation of the current kinematic model is its assumption of fixed cable lengths. In practice, the stainless steel cables undergo microscopic plastic deformation and experience minute stretching after repeated loading cycles. Even a tiny elongation on the order of 0.1\% can accumulate over the cable path, leading to a noticeable positional error at the end-effector, a phenomenon that compromises absolute accuracy and can manifest as a failure to reach a commanded pose.

To address this model inaccuracy, future work could move from a purely model-based control scheme to a data-driven, adaptive approach. One possible direction is the integration of Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms. An RL agent could be trained, either in a simulated environment or through extensive real-world operation, to learn the nonlinear relationship between motor commands and the resulting end-effector pose, thereby compensating for cable stretch and hysteresis in real time. By correlating commanded inputs with measured positions, potentially validated by external tracking systems during training, the model could update its compensation strategy throughout the instrument's service life rather than relying on a static kinematic model.

\subsection{Expanded Modularity and Context-Aware Operation}

The existing quick-swap mechanism provides a foundation for hardware modularity. The next step is to evaluate this capability by developing a library of specialized end-effector modules, such as bipolar forceps, needle holders, scissors, and custom jaws for specific procedures. This would allow the instrument to function as a modular multi-tool platform.

To use this modularity safely, the system would need to recognize an attached module and reconfigure its control logic accordingly. This could be achieved by embedding a Radio-Frequency Identification (RFID) tag within each module. Upon attachment, a reader in the main handle could identify the module and load the corresponding kinematic profile and software limits, such as maximum safe grasping force for delicate tissues. For additional verification, a miniaturized computer vision module could be integrated near the distal end. This would allow the system not only to identify the tool but also to visually confirm its state, such as blade open or closed, and its relative position to the surgical field, enabling semi-autonomous tasks and enhanced safety monitoring in future versions.

Together, these developments in intelligent control and context-aware modularity could make the instrument more precise, adaptable, and intuitive to use.

\subsection{Mechanical Reliability and Clinical Translation}

The current handheld instrument is best understood as a functional engineering prototype rather than a finalized clinical device. Future development should therefore include a structured reliability program. First, the cable transmission should be characterized over repeated winding and unwinding cycles to quantify cable stretch, guide-tube wear, pulley wear, and loss of pretension. Because the current design uses a 0.5\,mm 7$\times$37 tungsten wire cable routed through a Teflon guide tube and distal pulleys, fatigue and abrasion behavior should be measured under realistic bending radii and duty cycles. The resulting data would support maintenance intervals, replacement criteria, and improved routing geometry.

Second, the finite element analysis should be extended from static strength verification to design optimization. The updated FEA in this thesis indicates that the 6061-T4 jaw/tip and middle-base components remain below yield under conservative stall-torque-derived loading. However, clinical translation would require additional load cases, including off-axis tool contact, accidental collision, repeated cyclic loading, and worst-case clamping during misalignment. These studies should be combined with physical load tests so that the simulation model can be calibrated against measured deformation and failure thresholds.

Third, sterilization and biocompatibility must be addressed before any clinical evaluation. The current quick-swap concept is mechanically useful, but a surgical version would need sealed interfaces, cleanable surfaces, material compatibility with sterilization processes, and a separation between reusable handle components and disposable or sterilizable distal modules. This requirement may alter the material selection, cable termination design, sensor placement, and electrical connector layout. Future iterations should therefore treat sterilization and maintenance as primary design constraints rather than late-stage packaging details.

\subsection{Quantitative Validation Roadmap}

The next stage of validation should move beyond qualitative function tests toward a repeatable benchmark protocol. For the surgical instrument, relevant metrics include output angular accuracy, backlash, hysteresis width, tension repeatability, input-to-output latency, maximum sustainable grip force, and temperature rise during prolonged operation. These should be measured across representative commands such as repeated pitch-yaw sweeps, roll reversals, open/close cycles, and combined multi-DOF trajectories. Comparing these measurements before and after repeated cable cycles would directly quantify drift and wear.

For the input interface, future work should evaluate ergonomics and command consistency across users. A practical study could record command smoothness, overshoot, task completion time, and subjective fatigue while users perform standardized pick-and-place or peg-transfer tasks. Such experiments would clarify whether the mass distribution and input mapping proposed in this thesis actually reduce operator burden compared with a more conventional handheld arrangement.
